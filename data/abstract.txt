Background: In the field of biomedicine and personalized medicine, omics data, encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics have a very important role in understanding human biological processes. However, the presence of bias at various stages of the data lifecycle, from data acquisition to preprocessing and modeling, can significantly compromise the integrity of the research outcomes. Artificial intelligence (AI) is often regarded as a black box in medicine and biology. AI cannot deliver reliable or cost-effective precision medicine solutions unless supported by high-quality, bias-aware data. In future healthcare, where our goal is to reduce patient costs and side effects, it is essential to prepare good data and recognize sources of bias to build effective AI-driven algorithms in the omics field.

Objective: Because of these challenges, our objective is to identify the biases that may be introduced in omics data and analysis beyond representativeness. We are aware representativeness is the core issue of bias in omics, however, we want to study if there are other biases that threaten the validity of a study. Starting from data production, technical/ instrumental, computational/ analytical, and other challenges. Finally we propose methodologies aimed at mitigating or eliminating these biases, particularly within the context of employing AI in omics data analysis. Additionally, we propose mitigation strategies and introduce a tree-like structure of biases designed to guide researchers. This framework may serve as the foundation for a guideline, similar to TRIPOD-AI, that standardizes how omics research is conducted in the era of AI.

Method: We did a systematic literature search to identify review articles published between January 2019 and May 2024 in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. We also incorporated elements of the TRIPOD-AI guideline to address AI-based prediction models and highlighted key elements such as dataset description, preprocessing, bias, and calibration. The primary databases queried included PubMed and Scopus. Our search strategy employed a combination of Boolean operators and controlled vocabularies, such as MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms, particularly within PubMed. We also expanded our scope to include literature published in Chinese, in line with efforts to increase representational diversity in systematic reviews. One reviewer extracted the data and eight co-authors validated inclusion decisions through majority voting. After that, two reviewers reviewed all the included papers manually. 

Results: Using our search term strategy, we extracted 552 papers, and after deleting the duplicates and filtering the articles that we need to exclude, we got the final version of 179 articles that we separated into topics such as genomics, metabolomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, and general omics. We also added special emphasis to the biases present in Chinese articles as they are often understudied. The most prominent biases across all omics are sequencing technology limitations, sampling and recruitment, reference and annotation, and infrastructure and equity gaps. Importantly, our inclusion of Chinese-language studies uncovered additional context-specific biases often absent in English-language reviews. In microbiome research, biases were linked to dual nomenclature systems (ICNP vs. SeqCode), exclusion of uncultured species, and cultural/linguistic barriers in microbial taxonomy. In metabolomics, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) studies revealed database gaps for herbal compounds and reproducibility issues across laboratories, underscoring the need for tailored reference resources and standardized protocols.

Conclusion: Bias in omics research extends far beyond population non-representativeness, permeating technical platforms, reference standards, recruitment practices, and global infrastructure. Chinese literature uniquely highlights the intersection of scientific, cultural, and sociopolitical factors. Left unaddressed, these biases risk undermining scientific validity, slowing clinical translation, and exacerbating global health inequities. Mitigation requires a multipronged approach: diversifying datasets, investing in equitable infrastructure, adopting transparent AI methodologies, and embracing multilingual evidence bases. This study provides a preliminary guideline to help researchers design more robust, transparent, and equitable omics studies, ultimately supporting the development of reliable AI models and globally accessible precision medicine.
